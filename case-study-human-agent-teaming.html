Human-Agent Teaming in Complex Operational Environments

Researching how humans and autonomous agents can work together effectively on complex and dynamic tasks.

Role Lead Scientist / Human Factors Researcher
Focus Human-Agent Teaming
Methods Task Analysis, Scenario Design, Behavioral Research, Human Factors
Output Human-Agent Teaming Requirements & Research Frameworks

Overview

As autonomous systems become more capable, the central design question is no longer simply what the technology can do. It is how responsibility should be divided between the human and the agent, and how each teammate should respond when the other makes a mistake.

I studied human-agent teaming in complex operational environments to understand how tasks could be distributed between human operators and autonomous agents, what could go wrong, and how the human and agent could detect, communicate, and compensate for one another's failures.

Research principle: The goal was not to automate as much of the work as possible. The goal was to determine which responsibilities should remain with the human, which could be delegated to an agent, and what information and intervention mechanisms were necessary for the team to remain effective.

Research Foundations

The analysis was grounded in established human-automation and human-autonomy teaming research, particularly research on function allocation, levels of automation, human-agent interdependence, and the cognitive demands created when people supervise increasingly autonomous systems.

Function Allocation

Human-machine function allocation considers how system functions and tasks should be distributed between people and technology, including the interdependencies created by different allocations.

Roth et al. (2019)

Levels of Automation

Parasuraman, Sheridan, and Wickens (2000) distinguish different types and levels of automation across information acquisition, information analysis, decision/action selection, and action implementation.

Parasuraman, Sheridan & Wickens (2000)

Human-Autonomy Teaming

Human-autonomy teaming treats autonomous agents as team members with distinct roles working interdependently with humans toward a shared goal.

O'Neill et al. (2023)

These established concepts informed the project-specific frameworks and taxonomies used to analyze the operational scenarios.

The Problem

Autonomous systems can perform increasingly complex tasks, but simply assigning tasks to an agent does not guarantee that the overall human-agent team will perform better.

In dynamic environments, both teammates can have incomplete information, make errors, or misunderstand what the other is doing. A human may need to recognize that an agent has failed, understand why it failed, and intervene before the failure affects the larger task.

This creates a different design problem from conventional automation:

Task Allocation

Which tasks should be performed by the human, the agent, or jointly?

Shared Awareness

How does each teammate know what the other is doing, attempting to do, or unable to do?

Failure Recovery

How does the team detect, communicate, and recover when either teammate makes a mistake?

Framework 1: Human-Agent Task Allocation

The first framework organized the analysis around the distribution of work between human and agent. This was informed by the function-allocation literature, which emphasizes analyzing operational demands, alternative distributions of work, interdependencies, and tradeoffs between possible team configurations. (Roth et al., 2019)

For each task, I considered not only who performs the task, but also who is responsible for decision making, monitoring, and recovery.

Human-Led

The human performs the task and retains primary decision authority.

Agent-Led

The agent performs the task within defined operating conditions.

Collaborative

Human and agent contribute complementary capabilities to the same task.

Agent Recommends

The agent analyzes information or generates options while the human makes the decision.

Agent Executes

The agent performs the action while the human retains supervisory responsibility.

Adaptive Allocation

Responsibility can shift between human and agent as task conditions change.

Key insight: Task allocation is not simply a question of “human versus AI.” It can involve different allocations of sensing, analysis, decision making, action, monitoring, and recovery.

Framework 2: Human Control and Automation Management

The task-allocation analysis was complemented by established models of human interaction with automation. Parasuraman, Sheridan, and Wickens (2000) describe automation across different stages of information processing and across levels ranging from manual performance to highly automated operation. (Parasuraman et al., 2000)

Within this broader literature, different automation-management strategies change what the human is expected to do.

Human-Led Control

The human performs the task and the system primarily provides information or decision support.

Management by Consent

The automation proposes an action, but the human must explicitly authorize it before execution.

Olson & Sarter (2001)

Collaborative Control

Human and agent jointly contribute to the task, with responsibility distributed according to capability and context.

Management by Exception

The automation handles routine operations while the human intervenes when an exception or boundary condition requires human involvement.

Dekker & Woods (1999)

Highly Autonomous Operation

The agent performs actions with limited or no continuous human intervention within its defined operating envelope.

These modes should not be interpreted as a simple progression from “bad” to “good” or from “less AI” to “more AI.” The appropriate strategy depends on task demands, consequences of error, system reliability, human workload, and how readily the human can detect and respond to exceptions.

Human factors implication: Management by Exception can reduce the need for continuous human involvement, but it also places new demands on the human: detecting exceptions, maintaining sufficient awareness of system state, and taking effective control when intervention is required. Research has identified these intervention and situation-awareness challenges as important issues in supervisory control. (Dekker & Woods, 1999)

Research Approach

I approached the problem as a human-agent systems problem rather than as an automation problem.

  • Developed representative operational scenarios to examine how humans and autonomous agents would interact during complex tasks.
  • Conducted task analysis to identify human responsibilities, agent responsibilities, dependencies, information requirements, and potential failure points.
  • Considered both normal operations and situations in which either the human or agent could fail.
  • Identified opportunities for the human teammate to detect, intervene in, or compensate for agent failures.
  • Considered workload, situation awareness, trust, and information availability as factors affecting human intervention.

Framework 3: Human-Agent Failure & Recovery Taxonomy

A second project-specific taxonomy focused on what could happen when the team did not behave as expected.

Rather than treating “system failure” as a single category, I considered failures according to who failed, whether the failure was detected, and whether the other teammate could recover from it.

Agent Failure

The agent produces an incorrect, incomplete, or inappropriate action or recommendation.

Human Failure

The human makes an incorrect decision, misses information, or fails to act when action is required.

Detection Failure

A teammate fails to recognize that the other teammate has encountered a problem.

Coordination Failure

Human and agent form incompatible expectations about goals, actions, or responsibilities.

Intervention Failure

A problem is recognized, but the human or agent cannot intervene effectively.

Recovery

The team detects the problem, reallocates responsibility, and restores effective task performance.

Key question: If one teammate fails, does the other teammate have enough information, authority, time, and capability to detect the failure and compensate for it?

Designing for Failure, Not Just Success

A major part of the analysis focused on what happens when the system does not behave as expected.

For each scenario, I considered potential failures by either teammate and then examined whether the other teammate would have sufficient information and capability to recognize and respond to the failure.

This shifted the analysis from autonomous performance alone toward team-level resilience.

The broader human-autonomy teaming literature similarly emphasizes interdependence: humans and autonomous agents occupy distinct roles but depend on one another to achieve the team's common goal. (O'Neill et al., 2023)

Maintaining Shared Situation Awareness

Effective teaming requires more than giving the human an AI recommendation. The human needs an appropriate understanding of what the agent is doing, why it is doing it, and where its limitations may matter.

This led to consideration of information requirements such as:

  • What the agent currently believes or detects
  • What action the agent is taking or proposing
  • Why the agent selected that action
  • What information the agent is uncertain about
  • What conditions could cause the agent's recommendation to become unreliable
  • When human intervention is required

The appropriate level of information depends on the task. More information is not necessarily better if it increases workload or distracts the operator from the larger situation.

Framework 4: Calibrated Reliance

A separate framework addressed the relationship between system capability, human trust, and actual reliance on the agent.

The underlying principle is that successful automation should support appropriate reliance, rather than simply maximizing trust. The human's reliance should be consistent with what the system can actually do under the circumstances.

System Capability

How reliable is the agent for this task and under these conditions?

Human Understanding

Does the human understand the agent's capabilities and limitations?

Trust

How does the human's confidence in the agent develop?

Reliance

Does the human accept, verify, reject, or override the agent's output?

Outcome

What happens to task performance, errors, workload, and recovery?

Feedback

Does experience with the system update the human's understanding of its capabilities?

This approach is consistent with the human-automation trust literature, which emphasizes designing automation so that human reliance is appropriately calibrated to system capabilities and context. (Lee & See, 2004)

Workload and Intervention

Human involvement also has a cost.

If an autonomous system requires continuous monitoring, the human may become overloaded even when the agent performs most of the physical or computational work. Conversely, removing the human entirely may eliminate the judgment needed to handle unexpected conditions.

This is particularly important in management-by-exception systems. The human may spend less time actively performing the task while taking on a different cognitive role: monitoring system state, detecting exceptions, interpreting what happened, and deciding when to intervene. Research on automation has identified workload, situation awareness, complacency, and skill degradation as important human-performance considerations when designing automation. (Parasuraman, 2000)

The design therefore needs to consider when the human should:

  • Monitor the agent continuously
  • Review agent recommendations periodically
  • Be alerted only when intervention is needed
  • Take control of the task
  • Allow the agent to continue autonomously

Framework 5: Human Responsibility & Capability Preservation

The final framework connected task allocation with a broader human-factors question: what happens to human capability when increasingly capable systems take over portions of a task?

For each candidate task:

Can the agent perform it?
Should the agent perform it?
What must remain under human responsibility?
What must remain independently verifiable?
What human capability could be weakened through repeated delegation?

Delegation

Identify work that can be safely and effectively delegated to the agent.

Human Retention

Identify decisions, knowledge, or skills that should remain with the human.

Verification

Identify outputs that require meaningful human verification rather than passive acceptance.

Capability Effects

Consider how repeated delegation could change human knowledge, skill, judgment, or situation awareness.

This reflects a longstanding human-factors concern that automation can change human activity rather than simply remove it. The human may perform less of the original task while taking on new monitoring, coordination, and recovery responsibilities. (Parasuraman, 2000)

From Research to Design Requirements

The frameworks translated the human-agent analysis into requirements that could inform system design.

Responsibility

Clearly define which teammate is responsible for each task and decision.

Transparency

Provide enough information for the human to understand the agent's actions, limitations, and uncertainty.

Intervention

Give the human meaningful opportunities to intervene when the agent encounters conditions outside its capabilities.

Recovery

Design explicit mechanisms for detecting and recovering from human or agent errors.

Workload

Avoid creating a supervisory burden that offsets the benefits of automation.

Adaptation

Allow the division of responsibility to change as task conditions and system capabilities change.

Why This Matters for AI Product Research

Many AI product questions are framed around model performance: accuracy, latency, benchmark scores, or task completion.

Human-agent teaming introduces another layer of questions:

  • Will people understand what the system can and cannot do?
  • Will they know when to verify an AI recommendation?
  • Can they recognize when the AI is wrong?
  • Will the system create excessive monitoring or verification workload?
  • What human capabilities may be affected if the system takes over too much of the task?
  • How should responsibility be divided when both the human and AI contribute to an outcome?

These are fundamentally human factors and product research questions, not simply model-development questions.

How I Would Evaluate the Team

The frameworks provide hypotheses that can be tested empirically rather than treated as design assumptions.

A controlled evaluation could manipulate:

  • Agent reliability
  • Degree of autonomy
  • Automation-management strategy
  • Explanation and uncertainty information
  • Task complexity
  • Time pressure
  • Human workload
  • Failure severity

Behavioral measures could include:

  • Task performance
  • Error detection
  • Intervention timing
  • Reliance and override decisions
  • Recovery performance
  • Mental workload
  • Situation awareness
  • Trust calibration

This would allow the research to examine not only what people say about AI, but how they actually behave when deciding whether to rely on it.

Limitations and Next Steps

This portfolio case study presents the human-factors research and systems-analysis approach rather than claiming a completed empirical evaluation of a commercial AI system.

The frameworks shown here should therefore be understood as analytical tools informed by established research, not as newly validated scientific taxonomies.

A next phase would operationalize the frameworks into an experimental or prototype-based evaluation and test how different allocations of responsibility affect human and team performance.

Key Takeaway

The important question is not how much work AI can take over. It is how to design the human-agent team so that each teammate contributes what the other cannot reliably provide.

My human factors background provides a way to evaluate AI systems from that broader perspective: not only whether the technology works, but how it changes human decision making, workload, awareness, responsibility, reliance, and the ability to recover when things go wrong.
